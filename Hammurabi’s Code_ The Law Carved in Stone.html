282 laws, carved in stone for everyone to see

Around 1754 BCE, King Hammurabi of Babylon had his laws inscribed on a stone pillar and set up in public. It is one of the best-preserved law codes of the ancient world, and it changed how societies think about justice, contracts, and the duties of rulers.

The Stele of Hammurabi “Let the strong not harm the weak.”

A paraphrase of the stated aim in its prologue: to bring justice to the land and protect widows and orphans.

What is Hammurabi’s Code?

It is a collection of laws and legal decisions issued by Hammurabi, the sixth king of Babylon, who ruled from about 1792 to 1750 BCE. It was written in Akkadian cuneiform on a black diorite pillar, with a carving at the top of the king standing before Shamash, the sun god and god of justice.

Why was it created?

Hammurabi had conquered most of Mesopotamia. His kingdom now held many cities with different local customs, languages, and courts. He needed a shared standard to hold it together.

To unify an empire

Before Babylon’s rise, cities like Eshnunna and Isin each had their own rules. A single published code gave a growing state a common legal language and reinforced the king’s authority.

To show the king as just

The prologue and epilogue present Hammurabi as a shepherd of his people, chosen by the gods to establish justice. Publishing the law was also a way to win trust and legitimacy.

To make law predictable

Disputes over land, debts, marriages, and trade were growing as cities grew. Judges needed clear guidance, and ordinary people needed to know the rules.

To protect the vulnerable

The text states the goal of protecting widows, orphans, and the poor from abuse by the powerful, though it still favored people of higher rank.

The historical innovations

Hammurabi was not the first lawgiver. Earlier codes such as Ur-Nammu’s (about 2100 BCE) came first. But the Code’s scale, public display, and range of topics were new. Select each innovation to see what it was, why it mattered, and what it replaced.

Limits and common myths

It was not equal justice

Punishments depended on social class. Injuring a noble could bring a matching injury, while injuring a commoner or a slave usually meant a fine. Penalties could also be harsh, including death for many offenses.

“An eye for an eye” was not the whole code

Only some laws use equal-for-equal punishment. Many more set fines, wages, prices, and procedures for ordinary business.

Was it a law code or a set of rulings?

Historians debate this. Court records show judges rarely cited it directly, so it may have been more a monument of royal justice and a teaching text than a statute book consulted case by case.

Not the first, but influential

It copied and expanded older legal traditions. Scribes kept copying it for over a thousand years, which shows how highly it was valued.
